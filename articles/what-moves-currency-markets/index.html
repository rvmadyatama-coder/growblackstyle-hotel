What Moves Currency Markets?

Published: November 3, 2026 · Last reviewed: November 3, 2026

By GrowBlackStyle · 2026-10-29

Foreign-exchange prices express the value of one currency in another. A currency pair therefore compares two economies, not one. Exchange rates can respond to relative interest-rate expectations, inflation, growth, trade and capital flows, central-bank communication, political developments and changes in global risk appetite. These forces interact, and the market reaction depends partly on what investors already expected.

Interest-rate differentials

Investors compare expected returns across currencies, including interest rates and the risks of holding each asset. If the expected policy path in one economy rises relative to another, its currency may attract demand. But a higher rate does not guarantee appreciation: the increase may already be priced in, inflation may erode real returns, or investors may become more concerned about growth or fiscal risk.

Inflation and economic growth

Inflation can affect purchasing power and central-bank decisions. Growth data can influence expected corporate earnings, imports, investment flows and policy. A strong data release can support a currency if it improves the outlook, but may weaken it if it raises concern about overheating or policy instability. Interpret the release against expectations and the country's broader conditions.

Trade and capital flows

Exports and imports create demand for currencies, while cross-border investment creates financial-account flows. The balance of these flows can matter over time, but exchange rates also respond to portfolio allocation, hedging, borrowing and expectations. A trade surplus alone does not provide a reliable short-term forecast of currency appreciation.

Central-bank communication and intervention

Policy statements, meeting minutes, speeches and forecasts can shift expectations before a rate actually changes. Some central banks may intervene directly or use other policy tools, but the objectives, scale and effectiveness vary. Read official statements and distinguish an announced policy from market speculation about a possible action.

Risk sentiment and unexpected events

During periods of stress, investors may reduce exposure to riskier assets, seek liquidity or unwind carry trades. The currencies that benefit depend on the source of stress, funding positions and market structure. Political events, commodity shocks and sudden policy changes can produce gaps and wider spreads, especially in less liquid pairs.

Worked example: reading EUR/USD

EUR/USD states how many U.S. dollars one euro buys. If the quote moves from 1.0800 to 1.0900, the euro has appreciated against the dollar over that interval: one euro buys more dollars. It does not, by itself, tell you why the move occurred. To investigate, compare the latest euro-area and U.S. data, central-bank expectations, bond yields and relevant news over the same time window.

A repeatable currency research routine

  1. Identify the pair and the time horizon.
  2. Write down the base currency and quote currency correctly.
  3. Compare economic releases for both economies.
  4. Read the latest central-bank communication from both sides.
  5. Check relative yields, trade developments and risk sentiment.
  6. Record alternative explanations and what evidence would challenge the thesis.

Common mistakes

Do not analyse only the first currency in a pair. Do not confuse a quoted exchange rate with a percentage return on a leveraged position. Remember that spreads, rollover, financing and execution costs affect trading outcomes. A correct macroeconomic view can still lose money if timing, sizing or execution is poor.

A two-sided currency-pair analysis worksheet

Analyse both economies

For a pair such as EUR/USD, make a side-by-side record of policy-rate expectations, inflation, growth indicators, labour-market conditions and relevant official communications for both currency areas. A pair is a relative price: a positive development in one economy can be offset by an equally important change in the other.

Compare expectations, not just current levels

A current policy rate is not the same as the expected path of future rates. Identify what changed, when it changed and what the market had previously expected. A data release that appears strong in isolation may have little effect if it was already anticipated or if another part of the report is weak.

Check quote convention and data quality

EUR/USD is quoted as U.S. dollars per euro; USD/JPY is quoted as yen per U.S. dollar. A rise has a different interpretation depending on which currency is the base currency. Official reference rates can be useful for analysis but are not necessarily executable broker prices and may use a different fixing time.

Reader exercise

Choose a historical interval and record the pair’s start and end values, the relevant policy communications from both central banks, and the key releases from both economies. Separate facts from interpretation in the write-up. Do not infer a causal explanation from the exchange-rate chart alone.

Hypothetical rate-expectation scenario

Imagine markets revise the expected policy-rate path higher in the United States while the expected euro-area path remains unchanged. That change could support the dollar relative to the euro, all else equal. The exchange rate may nevertheless move differently if the change was already priced in or if growth, risk appetite and capital flows shift at the same time.

Repeatable currency research routine

  1. Write down the currency pair, direction and time horizon.
  2. Check the base and quote currency correctly.
  3. Compare the latest policy statements from both central banks.
  4. Compare inflation, employment and growth releases for both economies.
  5. Record relevant bond yields and their changes over the same time window.
  6. Check for political events, commodity shocks and broad risk sentiment.
  7. Write at least one alternative explanation and evidence that could challenge the thesis.

Important data limitation
